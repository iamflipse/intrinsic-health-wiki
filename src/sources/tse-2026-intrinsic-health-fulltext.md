What is health? This seemingly simple question is surprisingly hard to answer.

The World Health Organization defines it as “a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.” This definition is good as far as it goes, but it has major challenges: how do we measure health? What are the limits of health? If I am unhappy because I can’t afford a luxury car, is this a decrement to my well-being, and therefore my health?

In a recent publication with Martin Picard and other colleagues, we tried to tackle this problem. We argue that health can be defined not just as a vague aspiration, but as a property of our biological and psychological systems. It’s something that can be understood scientifically and ultimately quantified.

Imagine what it would mean to have a scientific way to measure health: You could see if your new diet is working for you. We could test medical interventions based not on whether they improve one or two symptoms, but on whether they benefit you overall. We could follow the health of populations in an objective way, assessing what policies are helping or harming us.

In a recent post here, Martin alluded to the need to approach health from a first principles perspective, and to understand how energy is part of health. 

In this post, we’ll take it a little further, digging into those first principles to try to build a coherent scientific framework to understand health empirically.

Health of the Body Versus Health of the Body in Its ContextImagine two 45-year-old women who are generally in “good health.” Both get into a car accident, and both become paraplegic.

One is a writer who has never been into sports very much. She lives in a city that is very accessible and wheelchair friendly. She is wealthy enough to afford changes to her lifestyle to accommodate her new physical state.

The other is passionate about marathon running and has constructed much of her identity around an active lifestyle. She lives in a mountainous, rocky area in a small town with very few wheelchair-accessible buildings. She is not wealthy and cannot afford to renovate her house, or to move.

Most of us would probably agree that the car accident had a bigger impact on the health of the second woman than the first, even though there might be no meaningful difference in their physiological states.

In other words, health is not just about the state of our body, but about the context that body is living in: our values, preferences, environment, culture, perceptions, behaviors, and lifestyle. We call this the “lens of context.”

Of course, each of us has a lens of context that is unique, and this is a huge obstacle for trying to come up with a universal, scientific understanding of the principles of health. So we need to make a distinction between intrinsic health (the state of our body as it permits health, generally speaking) and realized health (health as we experience it, via our own individual lens of context).

Source: Cohen et al. Science Advances 2025.Understanding realized health could be a whole academic discipline in itself. It is important, and perhaps this Substack will explore it in a future article. But here, we’ll focus on intrinsic health.

Where does intrinsic health come from? A First Principles PerspectiveRather than starting from a reductionist perspective, trying to build up a definition of health based on our understanding of the individual molecules and cells that make up our body, let’s tackle the question from a broader perspective. What is an organism? What does it mean to be alive, and how do organisms achieve this? The answer to these questions lies at the intersection of two fields: complex systems theory and evolutionary biology.

Complex systems theory is about the ways that all the components of our bodies (molecules, cells, tissues, organs, etc.) interact with each other, with feedback loops that mean you can’t always make simple predictions about how a change in one part of the system will affect things elsewhere.

For example, if I eat less because I’m trying to diet, I might lose weight. But I might also trigger my body to react to a shortage of incoming calories by both storing more fat and prompting a hunger response. I might inadvertently gain weight, due to my body’s natural mechanisms to self-adjust.

Complex systems theory is the study of many such systems—economies, ecosystems, weather systems, traffic networks, and, of course, our bodies. These systems have many interconnections and feedback loops, often making their behavior unpredictable.

Evolutionary biology adds another layer. It says, our bodies are not just complex systems. They are complex systems that have been optimized over billions of years for a reason: to propagate themselves. This optimization has led to fine-tuning of the networks in ways that help organisms survive and reproduce as well as possible.

While reproduction is of course very important in evolutionary biology, the first and most important task any organism has is to hold itself together: to survive long enough in this chaotic world that it might have a shot at reproducing.

In this perspective, an organism is a collection of molecules, cells, etc. that has been optimized to self-adjust to preserve its own internal dynamic state at least long enough to grow and reproduce.

This idea might not sound that radical—it may even sound obvious—but it is strongly at odds with the dominant biomedical paradigm. To understand why, consider the analogy of a tightrope walker.

The typical biomedical paradigm would argue that we can understand tightrope walkers and their balance by decomposing the system into its parts. For example, we might remark on the relationships between individual muscles involved in maintaining balance and note that when tightrope walkers fall, they tend to have a particular muscle in the left calf that is contracted. This might start the search for a drug to decontract that muscle in order to restore balance, without ever noticing that the contraction was in fact an attempt to restore balance.

Another key aspect of the tightrope walker is the dynamics of their muscles. Any tightrope walker who freezes all their muscles and becomes rigid will gradually tilt to one side and fall. On the other hand, a tightrope walker swinging wildly back and forth from left to right is also at high risk of falling.

The only way to maintain balance well is through microadjustments of many muscles happening all the time in an effort to maintain dynamic equilibrium. And indeed, this is what our bodies are doing every second of every day—not in two or three dimensions like the tightrope walker, but in thousands of dimensions as they adjust countless aspects of our biology to constantly changing conditions, inside and outside. Intrinsic health is thus our body’s capacity to maintain that dynamic equilibrium.

The Pillars of Intrinsic Health: Energy, Communication, and StructureWhat does it take for an organism to maintain intrinsic health? Again, tackling it from a first-principles perspective, we can identify the ingredients: energy, communication, and structure.

Energy is required to do any work, and there’s lots of work to be done. The second law of thermodynamics tells us that dispersal and disorder (known as entropy) increases in any closed system. In an open system, entropy can only be countered by energy inputs that help maintain information, structure, and order.

Without energy, our bodies would quickly stop functioning and decompose into their environments. Energy is the difference between a corpse and a living person.

However, sending 10,000 volts of energy into a corpse does not bring it back to life. The energy has to be guided, directed, and used in appropriate ways at appropriate moments in appropriate places. This guiding and directing requires a sophisticated internal decision-making apparatus that senses current conditions and sends energy or other resources where they are needed.

Communication is this decision-making apparatus, the software that makes our bodies run. It might seem like decision-making happens largely in the brain, and indeed the brain has a crucial role, but the decision making also happens within each cell. With molecular and bioelectric signaling happening at many scales, decision making that occurs throughout our bodies is often independent of the brain. Any information in the body that is shared and integrated to contribute to self-adjustment is part of communication—nearly every biochemical reaction is thus part of it.

However, communication and energy don’t happen in a vacuum. Again, context is key.

Energy is mediated by specific molecules (such ATP) and structures (such as our limb structure, which affects the energetic efficiency of movement). Communication also happens via specific molecules, structures, and pathways.

In other words, communication and energy need to be understood in the context of biological structures at different scales—whether it is the helical structure of DNA, organization of cells within a tissue, or the shape of anatomical structures such as blood vessels.

Structure is qualitatively different from energy and communication in that it is a limit on the others, or a context within which they occur. Nonetheless, it is impossible to understand an organism and how it maintains its health without considering how health is shaped by biological structures. And changes to these structures may affect health profoundly.

Source: Cohen et al. Science Advances 2025.Putting It All Together—Intrinsic Health as an Emergent PropertyThe energy, communication, and structure that are necessary for health don’t operate independently; they work together, synergistically.

Intrinsic health is not just the sum of its parts.

In much the same way that a battery, wire, and solenoid coil come together to generate a magnetic field that can’t be easily understood or predicted from the components, intrinsic health emerges from the dynamic interplay of energy, communication, and structure within the organism.

It is, in complex systems parlance, an emergent property. All this really means is that it cannot be easily understood by decomposing it into parts. In the same way that we cannot fully understand joy by studying neurons or fully understand weather by studying atoms, we can’t fully understand intrinsic health by just studying its elements separately.

This means that there is no specific state of each component that determines health (though some states may be incompatible with health). Rather, health results from the degree of coordination and self-adjustment that happens dynamically, as every aspect of the system responds to constant slight fluctuations. This suggests that health will best be measured not by a snapshot of the organism, but by the dynamic patterns of variation over time and in response to changing conditions.

Measuring Intrinsic HealthIn order for the concept of intrinsic health to gain scientific traction, we need to be able to measure it. But if we can’t break it down into constituent pieces and sum them up, how can we do this?

The answer again comes from complex systems theory. Stable, resilient systems—be they ecosystems, climate systems, or economies—tend to have specific dynamic signatures. Human biology shows the same patterns.

For example, we have shown that the same models that predict ecosystem collapse can be used to predict which hemodialysis patients are at highest risk of death, based not on the levels of blood markers but on their fluctuations over weeks and months. Again, the tightrope walker example is instructive. If we were to measure the contractions and decontractions of many muscles in a tightrope walker, we would easily detect characteristic signatures of someone keeping their balance well, versus someone swaying wildly back and forth.

There are also likely to be energetic signatures of health. In a healthy organism, energy is used efficiently and is broadly available when needed. Martin and his team’s work on the protein GDF15 and energy resistance shows that an imbalance in energy resistance creates key signatures that are broad markers of health status, and other signatures are likely to be discovered.

In the end, we don’t think there’s any one thing we’ll be able to measure that will precisely quantify intrinsic health. Rather, we think that we’ll identify many measures that capture part of the signal.

Some of them may measure energy, or communication, or structure. Some may measure what we call intrinsic health components—resilience, robustness, plasticity, performance, and sustainability. Yet others may integrate some or all of these elements.

We can then use various statistical techniques to figure out what all these measures have in common, and that is what we can call intrinsic health. Once we identify this gold standard measure, we will also need to identify simple, low-cost, and feasible ways to approximate it, and validate that these measures do indeed strongly predict all the health outcomes we care about: mortality and chronic diseases, but also quality of life, physical function, cognitive function, and independence.

Source: Cohen et al. Science Advances 2025.What Does It Mean For You?The intrinsic-realized health framework gives us a simple, intuitive, and theoretically justified way to understand many key notions in health.

Does health decline with age? Well, realized health might not if the contextual factors are right, but intrinsic health will certainly tend to decline.

Does having a cold impact your health? Well, as long as you recover fully, your intrinsic health never changes—your immune response did exactly what it should all through the process. But your realized health might have suffered in the meantime.

And how does trauma affect health? Early-life trauma can get biologically embedded, leading to long-term decrements in both intrinsic and realized health, though both might be modified with appropriate treatment.

Source: Cohen et al. Science Advances 2025.Intrinsic health is not just useful at a conceptual level, though. It should also be measurable, as I described earlier. While it will take some time to derive and validate good measures, there is every reason to think this effort will succeed.

And once we have measures, we will have a single target to use in health studies. No more wondering or worrying  about how coffee is good for heart disease, but bad for cancer, but good for dementia, but only if you stand on your left foot holding your nose while whistling. (I’m exaggerating, of course, but there’s a grain of truth there.) We can simply ask how coffee consumption impacts intrinsic health—across people in general, or for you specifically. Or how your new exercise regimen is working for you. Or whether subsidizing fresh produce at grocery stores improves the health of communities. Pick your question, and it’s now easier to answer…

Lastly, the conceptual work behind intrinsic health is part of a movement.

This movement is challenging the traditional biomedical model that we can understand health by defining diseases, or that we can understand organisms by breaking them down into their smallest parts and seeing how they interconnect, statically.

It challenges the notion that there is a pill for everything, and that improving health means identifying more pills we should take every day. It will position health as something that can be measured along a gradient, and something we can work to build, preserve, and restore across the entire life course, preventing diseases and maintaining quality of life at all ages.
